01 — Chữ Nôm

A script built to write Vietnamese.

Chữ Nôm is a historical Vietnamese writing system, built from Chinese characters that were adapted, combined, and repurposed to record spoken Vietnamese — not classical Chinese.

For centuries it sat alongside Hán (classical Chinese) as one of the ways Vietnamese was written down, before the Latin-based quốc ngữ script became the national standard in the 20th century.

It is this hybrid script — Chinese in shape, Vietnamese in sound and meaning — that appears throughout the book at the center of this story.

Title page, Technique du peuple Annamite, Henri Oger, 1908–1909
02 — The Book

Technique du peuple Annamite.

Between 1908 and 1909, French scholar Henri Oger worked in Hanoi with local artisans to document daily life in Vietnam — trades, tools, gestures, and street scenes — through thousands of hand-drawn illustrations.

Each drawing was carved into a wooden block and printed by hand, then numbered and, in many cases, labelled in Chữ Nôm. The result, Technique du peuple Annamite, remains one of the largest visual records of ordinary Vietnamese life from the early 20th century.

Surviving copies are held in libraries and museum collections. The plate referenced on this page is reproduced from the historical work; it is not a TKG creation.

03 — The Image

One page, drawn by hand.

Plate 592 · Technique du peuple Annamite
Henri Oger, Hanoi, 1908–1909

Among the thousands of figures Oger's artisans carved and printed, one shows a person carrying two woven baskets balanced on a shoulder pole — a way of carrying and selling goods door to door that was common on the streets of early 20th-century Vietnam.

Three characters are printed directly above the figure: 半遶薑.

It is not a literal, character-by-character dictionary translation; it reflects what the drawing depicts, read together with its caption. The image, the characters, and this context belong together.

05 — What the Image Tells Us

Ginger, carried by hand.

The drawing documents something ordinary: ginger, sold fresh, carried directly from seller to household. It was not a specialty item or a curiosity — it was produce, moved through the everyday rhythm of a Vietnamese street.

Ginger is also the lead ingredient in Morning Ember, one half of Combo Ember. That connection is why TKG chose this plate for the box — not as a claim of unbroken lineage between a 1909 drawing and a 2026 product, but as a way of placing the ingredient in its own history.

We are not reproducing the past. We are carrying a trace of it forward.
