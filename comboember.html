Hanoi, c. 1908–1909

Before ginger became a TKG product, it was already part of everyday Vietnamese life.

02 — The Image

Technique du peuple Annamite.

Between 1908 and 1909, French scholar Henri Oger worked in Hanoi with local artisans to document daily life in Vietnam — trades, tools, gestures, and street scenes — through thousands of hand-drawn illustrations.

Each drawing was carved into a wooden block and printed by hand, then numbered and, in many cases, labelled in Chữ Nôm. This image, of a ginger seller and a short line of script beside him, is one of them.

Plate 592, Technique du peuple Annamite, Henri Oger, Hanoi, 1908–1909
The Inscription

A line of Nôm script, recorded beside the image.

03 — A Vietnamese Script

Not Chinese. Nôm.

If you read Chinese, this may look familiar — but this is Nôm, a Vietnamese writing system.

Nôm is the writing system; Vietnamese is the language it records.

Nôm is a historical Vietnamese writing system used to write Vietnamese. It draws on Chinese characters, borrowing some directly and adapting or creating others to represent Vietnamese words and their readings.

For centuries, Nôm was used across Vietnamese literature, history, religion, administration, and everyday life. That is why an old Vietnamese inscription can look familiar to a reader of Chinese, while actually recording Vietnamese.

One character leaves us with a question.

In Nôm, characters could be used for their meaning, their sound, or both. Here, that distinction matters:

04 — Ginger Street Peddler

Ginger Street Peddler

This is how TKG names the image today.
Not a literal translation. An interpretation.

The original inscription belongs to its historical context. "Ginger Street Peddler" is our contemporary way of describing what the image represents to us: a person carrying and selling ginger as part of everyday Vietnamese commerce.

A contemporary name for an old image.
